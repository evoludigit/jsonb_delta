# jsonb_delta v0.2.0 — measured performance, Hetzner CCX13

**This artifact supersedes the figures in `docs/PERFORMANCE.md` and `README.md`.**
Those figures are not reproduced by this measurement. Most of them are wrong in
magnitude, several are wrong in direction, and the trend they describe runs
opposite to the one that occurs.

Issue #15 asked for numbers keyed to a version and a machine profile that a third
party can rent and re-run. This is that: every row below comes from a €0.07/hour
instance anyone can create, and the raw per-trial timings are committed alongside.

Dedicated vCPU, not shared: steal time at the ~1 ms scale being measured is not a
rounding error, it is the whole signal.

**The matrix was run twice on the same host.** Median run-to-run drift on the
ratio was 2.7%, worst case 15.8% (confined to the largest ratios and the smallest
absolute times). Figures below are the mean of the two runs. All 49 scenarios
reported `outputs_match = true` — the harness withholds a ratio between arms that
produce different documents, and the only rows it withheld were the two
calibration controls designed to disagree.

Reproduce with `scripts/provision_bench_vps.sh up && … run && … down`.

## Result 1 — the published 2–7× does not hold, and inverts with array size

Native baselines are transcribed from `docs/PERFORMANCE.md` itself (jsonb_set +
jsonb_agg re-aggregation), so this compares against the document's own claim, not
a strawman. Ratios > 1 mean jsonb_delta is faster.

| Operation | 10 elem | 50 elem | 100 elem | 1000 elem | Published |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| update, in-memory | 1.56× | 1.09× | 0.93× | **0.67×** | 2–3× |
| update, stored table | 1.36× | 1.00× | 0.91× | **0.77×** | 2–3× |
| delete, in-memory | 1.53× | 1.03× | 0.90× | **0.66×** | 5–7× |
| delete, stored table | 1.47× | 0.96× | 0.88× | **0.77×** | 5–7× |

Text keys measured within noise of integer keys throughout (e.g. update 1000
in-memory: 0.65× text vs 0.67× int), so the table above holds for both.

Three things follow, and none of them are marginal:

1. **The claims are not met at any array size measured.** The best case anywhere
   in the sweep is 1.56×, against a published 2–3×; the delete claim of 5–7× is
   overstated by roughly a factor of four at its best and by an order of magnitude
   at 1000 elements.
2. **At realistic sizes jsonb_delta is _slower_ than native SQL** for these
   operations — 0.66×–0.77× at 1000 elements, i.e. native is ~1.4× faster.
3. **The published trend runs backwards.** README.md:135-137 reports the ratio
   *rising* with array size (2.0× → 3.2× → 3.6× at 10 → 100 → 1000). It *falls*
   (1.56× → 0.93× → 0.67×). This is what the #15 reporter's mechanical argument
   predicts: whole-document parse/serialize dominates and both arms pay it, so
   faster element matching is a shrinking share of a total it cannot move.

The reporter was right, and their "parity" reading was if anything generous.

## Result 2 — two claims are directionally wrong

| Function | Measured | Published |
|---|---|---|
| `jsonb_array_update_where_batch` (10 updates, 100 elem) | **0.94×** | 3–5× |
| `jsonb_merge_shallow` (50-element doc) | **0.34×** | 1.5–2× |
| `jsonb_merge_shallow` (1000-element doc) | **0.15×** | 1.5–2× |

`jsonb_merge_shallow` is **3–7× slower than the native `||` operator** while being
advertised as 1.5–2× faster. Both arms were verified byte-identical, so this is a
real result and not a semantic mismatch. `||` is a built-in operating on jsonb's
binary representation; the extension function pays a full parse and re-serialize
to do the same job. This claim should be withdrawn outright rather than adjusted.

## Result 3 — coalescing is the real win, and it is large

The same host, measuring `jsonb_apply_changeset` (PR #16) against a chain of N
`jsonb_smart_patch_array` calls:

| Array | N ops | Ratio |
|---|---|---|
| 200 | 1 | **0.96×** (control — parity, as it must be) |
| 500 | 5 | 4.74× |
| 500 | 20 | 22.95× |
| 500 | 50 | 52.39× |
| 5000 | 50 | **71.17×** |

This is the one part of the project's performance story that survives
measurement, and it is stronger here than on a developer workstation (41× → 71×
at 5000/50) because the chain's repeated serialization costs more on slower cores.

The N=1 row is the control that makes the rest credible: at one op both arms do
exactly one parse/serialize, and the ratio is parity.

**But it must not be quoted alone.** Against `jsonb_array_update_where_batch`,
which already does N integer-keyed updates in a single pass, the changeset is at
parity: **1.02× / 0.99× / 1.01×**. Its advantage over `batch` is coverage —
heterogeneous ops, several paths in one call, and UUID/text match keys, which
`batch` cannot express at all (it reads `match_value` via `as_i64` and silently
skips anything else).

## What this means

The mechanism explains every row: **jsonb_delta wins where it removes a
serialization, and only there.**

- Replacing one native re-aggregation with one extension call removes no
  serialization — both sides parse and re-serialize the document once. Hence
  parity, and worse than parity once the extension's own overhead is counted.
- Replacing N chained calls with one changeset removes N−1 serializations. Hence
  ratios that track N.

So the headline should not be a speedup multiplier on individual operations at
all. It is: *coalescing many edits into one pass is a large win; individual
surgical operations are at parity with well-written native SQL, and slower on
large arrays.*
